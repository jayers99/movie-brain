## Reading list

1. **_Film Art: An Introduction_ — Bordwell & Thompson.** The foundation: the physical
   grammar of cinema (editing, mise-en-scène, lighting, cinematography), not abstract theory.
2. **_Film_ — Ronald Bergan.** Friendly surface-level companion to read alongside.
3. **_Screenwriting: The Sequence Approach_ — Paul Gulino.** The one book for analyzing
   scripts (pairs with the 15-minute-sequence method below).

## Method (how to watch)

- **Train the eyes**: actively scan the whole frame — margins, layers, doorways, prop
  placement; consciously register every cut.
- **Two-Watch analysis**: first watch = general impressions (visual style, editing speed,
  camera behavior, staging); second watch = close reading with timestamped "reminders",
  mapping the film's architecture into ~15-minute sequences, noting common vs. rare shots.
- **The No-Hogwash rule**: judge only the physical reality of the screen. "If the shot
  doesn't speak for itself, it probably has nothing to say." No unfalsifiable symbolism, no
  guessing intent.

## The syllabus — seven progressive units

Each unit sequences Moviewise videos introductory → advanced.

1. **Visual composition & the frame** — aspect ratios → camera movement, mirrors, off-screen
   space → *Nosferatu*'s look, "The Most Brilliant Shot in Movie History", horror-camera tricks.
2. **Directing & staging** — "Why Nobody Knows What Film Directors Do", "This Is How You
   Analyse Film Directing" → the two types of directing, actor positioning, Spielberg's
   attention-direction → invisible directing, old-master staging.
3. **Scene construction** — the most useful scene type, "How to Write The Perfect Scene" →
   failed vs. triumphant scene mechanics (*Spider-Man 2*) → "The Moment That Made a
   Masterpiece".
4. **Screenwriting & structure** — witty dialogue, intelligent characters → dialogue rhythm,
   ensemble casts → "Behold the Perfect Screenplay", theme in *Unforgiven*, delivering
   messages without didacticism.
5. **Editing & pacing** — why long films feel short → "This is as good as editing gets" →
   long takes and one-shot construction.
6. **Acting** — can a great actor save a line? → what makes a great performance → ensembles
   as collaboration.
7. **Critical synthesis — what makes a film good** — "A Guide for Analyzing Movies",
   *Casablanca* → judging the Oscars, fun-vs-good tension → the greatest-films canon and its
   missteps.

## The good-direction framework (his objective criteria)

Priority of the shot (judge what's on screen, not intent) · aesthetic merit as synthesis of
parts · staging/blocking · **invisible directing** (technique hidden behind story) ·
**camera motivation** (following a center of attention vs. aimless seeking) · **geographic
coherence** (never losing the viewer spatially) · the strong frame (information-dense
composition) · **z-axis depth** (layers, not "clothesline" blocking) · visual convenience ·
aperture framing (frames within frames) · meticulous effort over point-and-shoot.

## The 8-dimension analysis rubric (→ the app's practice loop)

Post-watch rubric, fillable in ~10 minutes, each dimension scored 1–5 (sum = "objective
craftsmanship score" /40, from *under-directed* to *classical master*):

1. Staging & blocking (z-axis, layering)
2. Camera motivation (following vs. seeking)
3. Spatial / geographic coherence
4. Composition & the strong frame (aperture framing, visual convenience)
5. Dialogue & subtext (indirect approach, "opinions of opinions")
6. Structure & scene economy ("exposition as ammunition")
7. Editing & pacing (periodic vs. loose long takes)
8. Acting & ensemble dynamics (existing in the moment)
