# The Attributed-Critic Corpus

A film-criticism tutor needs critics. This page says in what shape, and what the tutor may do with them: assemble a corpus of attributed critics, keep their disagreements intact, retrieve without authoring, and run an argument rather than a score.

## Why a corpus replaces "read one critic"

The first instinct is a single-critic rule: the learner reads exactly one professional per film, because averaging five critics gives consensus mush. The instinct protects something real — an average has no commitments and cannot be cross-examined. But it conflates two proposals. Averaging critics into a blend is one. Coverage is the other: gather enough of the critical field that whatever scene the learner raises, somebody has written about that scene, and the tutor can answer with an attributed position instead of an improvisation. The single-critic rule was aimed at the first proposal; the tutor needs the second.

What survives from the old rule is its reason: **preserve the disagreement, never launder it.**

## A corpus is coverage, not consensus

The corpus is assembled as a fault-line map, never as a summary.

| Element | What it holds |
|---|---|
| The canon case | Why the film is defended — attributed and quotable |
| The strongest dissent | A named critic who thinks it falls short, and exactly where |
| The collision | The specific points where the two positions contradict each other |

The collision is the payload. The characteristic case is the same observation carrying opposite signs: the canon case reads a film's mixed tonal registers as design, and a named dissenter reads the identical mixing as the flaw. A fused summary erases exactly that into "widely acclaimed" — the one sentence nobody can argue with, and therefore the one that trains nothing. Why canon claims are contestable at all is the subject of [[film-canon-epistemology]].

## Retrieval is not authorship

Loading the tutor with criticism fixes the information asymmetry in one direction only: the tutor gets claims about the film, never the film. A loaded tutor can sound authoritative about a shot it cannot see, so the gap needs rules rather than a denial.

1. Tag every scene claim sourced or inferred. The tutor never asserts that a shot exists on its own authority.
2. The learner speaks first on any scene the learner raises. The read enters the record before the corpus opens on it — the per-scene form of the sealed recall in [[film-study-protocol]].
3. The tutor retrieves and attributes; it does not synthesise a verdict of its own and present it as the field's. A position without a name attached is not in the corpus.

The third rule is the line between a tutor and a critic. **The corpus has authors; the tutor has an index.**

## The debate that replaced scoring-as-training

The four buckets of [[film-study-protocol]] measure the learner's eye against the corpus. Measurement is not training: being scored on recall is a scoreboard, not a game. The game is a Socratic debate in which learner and tutor define and sharpen arguments and attack the attributed critics' positions — the tutor delivering the critics' cases, not inventing them. The reusable moves:

- Attack the reasoning, not the verdict. A conclusion may be right for reasons that would cost the learner films never willingly given up. The verdict is the learner's to hold; the argument underneath it is what gets trained.
- The falsifiability check. Put to any defence: what would the film have to do for this defence to be wrong? A defence nothing could count against is an immunity card. Force it to make a checkable prediction — which the learner can test on the film and the tutor cannot.
- The consistency test. Apply the criterion just used to eject a film to a film the learner already likes; it makes the criterion pay a cost the learner can feel.
- Catch the rescue. When a consistency test bites, the reflex is a distinction that saves the liked film. Check whether it is a difference of kind or of degree; a rescue that contradicts a verdict from the same session is the highest-value catch available.
- Point at the better answer; do not hand it over. Often the learner has already said the stronger version and then reached for the weaker one. Show the walk-past.
- Withdraw fast and say so. Naming a dead objection is what makes the live ones credible.
- Never ask the learner to defend against evidence the learner does not have. A challenge built on an unwatched film is not a Socratic question.

## The criterion is the long game

The most durable thing the debate produces is not a verdict but the learner's own admission criterion — a stated bar for what a film must do to earn the canon. Treat it as a live hypothesis across the whole programme, with two cautions. A reader-response bar measures the encounter rather than the object, so it is a sorting rule for certain and a canon claim only if the learner can pay for the promotion. And a criterion abstracted from two films rests on n=2; wait for three real examples, and retest hardest where the canon disagrees with the learner. Whether such a bar can be taught at all is the question of [[can-taste-be-tutored]], [[taste]], and [[aesthetics-lineage]].
